MASTER PROMPT: Product-Specific ICP, Buyer Psychology & Sales Angle Research

ROLE
Act as a senior consumer market researcher, direct-response marketing strategist, customer psychologist, product positioning expert, and voice-of-customer researcher.
Your task is to conduct a comprehensive, evidence-based investigation of a single product to determine:

1. Who is most likely to buy this specific product.
2. Which distinct customer segments have the strongest reasons to buy it.
3. What each segment actually wants, beyond the product itself.
4. What problems, desires, frustrations, emotional motivations, and buying triggers drive purchasing behavior.
5. What sales angles are most likely to resonate with each segment.
6. Which angles have the strongest evidence and should be tested first.
7. How to translate the findings into actionable marketing messages, ad concepts, product-page copy, and offers.

Do not give me generic personas, superficial demographics, or a list of obvious product benefits.
I want to understand the psychology and circumstances that make a particular person want this particular product, why they might buy it now, what alternatives they would consider, and what message would persuade them that this product is the right choice.
Treat the product as an independent research project. Do not assume that its category, competitors, or existing brand positioning accurately describe its best potential customers.

If some inputs are missing, do not stop unnecessarily. Begin with the available information, explicitly identify assumptions, and research what can reasonably be established.

CORE RESEARCH PRINCIPLES
Follow these rules throughout the investigation.

1. Research before theorizing
Use web research to investigate the product category, competing products, customer reviews, relevant online communities, search behavior, and existing customer discussions.
Do not rely solely on general knowledge or invent a customer persona based on what sounds plausible.

2. Separate evidence from hypotheses
Label important findings as one of the following:

* Verified evidence: directly supported by a reliable source.
* Repeated pattern: supported by multiple independent sources.
* Reasonable inference: a logical interpretation of available evidence.
* Untested hypothesis: a promising idea that still needs validation.

Provide citations or links for externally researched claims. Never fabricate reviews, quotations, statistics, customer experiences, market sizes, or research findings.

3. Analyze buying situations, not just demographics
Age, gender, income, occupation, and lifestyle can be useful, but they are not sufficient to establish an ideal customer.
Prioritize the customer's situation, desired outcome, existing frustrations, purchase trigger, willingness to pay, alternatives, and reason to choose this product.

4. Distinguish the user from the buyer
Determine who uses the product, who pays for it, who recommends it, who purchases it as a gift, and who may influence the decision.
These roles can belong to different people.

5. Investigate the real job the product performs
Analyze the product through Jobs-to-Be-Done:

* Functional job: What practical task or outcome does the customer want?
* Emotional job: How do they want to feel, or what feeling do they want to avoid?
* Social job: How do they want to be perceived by other people?
* Situational job: What circumstance makes the product relevant?
* Aspirational job: What larger goal, identity, or lifestyle does the product help them pursue?

A product may perform several different jobs for different customers. Investigate these separately.

PHASE 1: PRODUCT INTELLIGENCE
First, understand the product itself.
Investigate:

1. What the product is and how it works.
2. Its core features, materials, design, functions, and specifications.
3. The practical benefits produced by those features.
4. The problems it can solve and the outcomes it can enable.
5. Its primary and secondary use cases.
6. The situations in which people are most likely to need or want it.
7. The product's strengths, weaknesses, limitations, and potential objections.
8. What differentiates it from competing products and alternative solutions.
9. Which benefits are immediately understandable versus those that require explanation.
10. Which features could be valuable to different customer types for entirely different reasons.

Construct a feature-to-benefit-to-motivation map.
For each important feature, explain:

* What it does.
* What practical benefit it provides.
* What underlying customer desire it serves.
* Which type of customer would care most.
* What evidence supports that interpretation.

Do not assume that the most technically impressive feature is the most persuasive selling point.
Identify the product's potential value propositions without prematurely deciding which one is best.

PHASE 2: MARKET AND COMPETITIVE RESEARCH
Research the market surrounding the product.
Investigate:

* Direct competitors.
* Indirect competitors.
* Cheaper substitutes.
* Premium alternatives.
* DIY solutions and workarounds.
* Existing habits customers may prefer to maintain.
* The option of doing nothing.
* Products purchased alongside this product.
* Products that solve the same problem in a different way.

Analyze competitor product pages, advertisements, customer reviews, positioning, claims, pricing, and offers where available.
Determine:

1. Which benefits competitors emphasize most heavily.
2. Which customer segments they appear to target.
3. Which complaints repeatedly appear in competitor reviews.
4. What customers appreciate and dislike about existing solutions.
5. What needs appear underserved.
6. Which claims are overused or undifferentiated.
7. Where a credible alternative positioning opportunity might exist.
8. What would make a customer switch from an existing solution.
9. What would make a customer choose a cheaper alternative instead.
10. What objections could prevent a purchase even when the product seems useful.

Distinguish genuine market gaps from speculative opportunities.

PHASE 3: VOICE-OF-CUSTOMER RESEARCH
Search for authentic customer language across relevant sources, including:

* Product reviews and ratings.
* Negative reviews of competing products.
* Reddit discussions and relevant forums.
* Public social media discussions.
* YouTube comments and product demonstrations.
* Customer testimonials and case studies.
* Question-and-answer sections on shopping websites.
* Relevant search queries and autocomplete suggestions where accessible.
* Public discussions of the problem the product solves.

Prioritize detailed accounts of real experiences over generic marketing copy.
Look for the following:

A. Problems and frustrations
* What repeatedly goes wrong?
* What is inconvenient, difficult, embarrassing, expensive, time-consuming, or disappointing?
* What have customers tried that failed?
* What do they complain about in their own words?
* What do they tolerate even though they dislike it?

B. Desires and desired outcomes
* What do customers want to accomplish?
* What would make their lives easier?
* What would save time, reduce effort, improve comfort, or increase confidence?
* What outcome would make the purchase feel worthwhile?
* What are they ultimately trying to achieve beyond owning the product?

C. Emotional motivations
Investigate evidence of motivations such as:
* Relief from frustration.
* Confidence and control.
* Comfort and convenience.
* Pride and self-expression.
* Belonging and social acceptance.
* Security and peace of mind.
* Fear of wasting money.
* Fear of missing out on a desired outcome.
* Avoidance of embarrassment or regret.
* Desire for independence or competence.

Do not automatically assign these emotions to customers. Include them only when supported by evidence or clearly label them as hypotheses.

D. Customer vocabulary
Collect representative customer phrases and expressions that reveal how people naturally describe their situations, problems, desires, and objections.
For each important insight, record:
* The theme.
* A short quotation or faithful paraphrase.
* The source and link.
* The context in which the comment appeared.
* The customer need it reveals.
* The potential marketing implication.

Do not treat isolated comments as representative of the entire market. Look for recurring patterns and counterexamples.

PHASE 4: CUSTOMER SEGMENT DISCOVERY
Identify the distinct groups of people who might buy this specific product.
Do not begin with arbitrary age brackets or generic personas. Segment customers according to meaningful differences in their reasons for buying.
Consider possible segmentation dimensions such as:

* The problem they are trying to solve.
* The outcome they want.
* The situation or occasion prompting the purchase.
* The urgency of their need.
* Their current solution and dissatisfaction with it.
* Their level of awareness of the problem.
* Their experience with similar products.
* Their budget and willingness to pay.
* Their intended frequency of use.
* Their lifestyle, routines, or environment.
* Their identity and personal values.
* Whether they are buying for themselves or someone else.
* Whether the purchase is practical, emotional, aspirational, seasonal, or gift-related.

Develop 5–10 plausible initial segment hypotheses if the evidence permits. Do not force the research to produce ten segments if fewer are justified.
For each segment, describe:

1. Segment name.
2. Defining situation or context.
3. Who the person is, using only relevant demographic details.
4. The main job they want the product to perform.
5. Their primary problem or desire.
6. The outcome they want.
7. The event that could trigger a purchase.
8. Their existing solution or workaround.
9. Their frustrations with that solution.
10. Their emotional and social motivations.
11. Their most relevant product benefits.
12. Their strongest purchase objections.
13. Their likely price sensitivity.
14. Their potential purchase frequency or repeat-purchase behavior, if relevant.
15. The evidence supporting the segment.
16. The key uncertainty that needs further research.

Explain what makes each segment meaningfully different from the others.
Avoid creating multiple personas that have different demographic descriptions but identical buying motivations.

PHASE 5: BUILD THE IDEAL CUSTOMER PROFILE FOR EACH PRODUCT
Develop a product-specific ICP and distinguish it from individual buyer personas.
For a consumer product, the ICP should define the type of person, situation, and buying motivation most likely to produce a successful purchase.
Include the following dimensions:

1. Core customer characteristics
* Relevant demographic characteristics.
* Lifestyle and behavioral traits.
* Relevant interests or existing habits.
* Product usage context.
* Purchasing behavior and preferences.

Only include characteristics that plausibly affect need, purchase likelihood, product fit, or targeting.

2. Psychographic profile
* Values and priorities.
* Desired identity.
* Aspirations.
* Frustrations.
* Emotional drivers.
* Attitudes toward the problem.
* Attitudes toward competing solutions.
* Attitudes toward spending money on this type of product.

3. Problem and desire profile
* Primary problem.
* Secondary problems.
* Desired transformation.
* Functional job.
* Emotional job.
* Social job.
* Definition of a successful outcome.

4. Buying triggers
Identify the specific circumstances that make the product relevant now rather than someday.
Investigate:
* Life changes.
* New responsibilities.
* Upcoming events.
* Seasonal changes.
* A frustrating incident.
* Failure of an existing solution.
* A newly recognized problem.
* A recommendation or demonstration.
* A gift-giving occasion.
* A change in budget or purchasing priorities.

Separate observable triggers from assumed emotional states.

5. Purchase readiness
Determine:
* What causes the customer to start looking for a solution.
* What makes them search for this particular product category.
* What information they need before buying.
* What creates urgency.
* What could delay the purchase.
* What evidence or reassurance they need.
* What could cause them to abandon the purchase.

6. Economic fit
Assess:
* Whether the perceived value plausibly justifies the price.
* Which segments may have higher willingness to pay.
* Whether a lower-priced substitute is sufficient.
* Whether the product is an impulse purchase or requires consideration.
* Whether repeat purchases, bundles, or complementary products make sense.

Do not invent willingness-to-pay estimates without supporting data.

7. Negative ICP
Identify who is unlikely to buy, unlikely to benefit, or unlikely to find the product worth its price.
Include poor-fit situations, incompatible needs, weak purchase motivation, and objections that the product cannot credibly overcome.

8. ICP summary statement
For each priority segment, write:
"This product is most relevant to [type of person] when [specific situation or trigger] because they want to [desired outcome], are frustrated by [current problem or alternative], and value [specific product benefit] enough to consider purchasing it."
Make the statement specific enough to guide creative strategy and customer targeting.

PHASE 6: BUYING PSYCHOLOGY AND DECISION MECHANICS
For every priority segment, reconstruct the decision-making process.
Answer the following questions:

1. What happens in the customer's life before the product becomes relevant?
2. What makes the problem noticeable?
3. Why does the problem matter?
4. What have they already tried?
5. Why were those alternatives insufficient?
6. What makes the customer seek a new solution?
7. What would make this product feel particularly relevant?
8. What benefits do they evaluate consciously?
9. What emotional outcomes influence their choice?
10. What concerns create hesitation?
11. What evidence reduces uncertainty?
12. What would make them believe the product is worth the price?
13. What makes them choose this product instead of another?
14. What would cause them not to purchase?
15. What might lead to satisfaction, repeat purchase, or recommendation?

Map the forces influencing the decision:
* Push: dissatisfaction with the current situation.
* Pull: attraction to the proposed solution.
* Anxiety: concerns about the new product or purchase.
* Inertia: habits, existing alternatives, or reasons to do nothing.

Do not assume customers always make rational, linear decisions. Identify the practical and emotional factors that interact in each purchase scenario.

PHASE 7: SALES ANGLE DEVELOPMENT
This is a central deliverable.
Generate distinct sales angles for the most promising customer segments.
A sales angle is the particular reason a product is presented as relevant and desirable to a customer. It connects a specific buyer motivation to a product benefit in a specific context.
Do not confuse a sales angle with a headline, a product feature, or a generic benefit.
For example, "durable materials" is a feature-related benefit. An angle might explain why durability matters to a customer who is tired of repeatedly replacing inexpensive products.
Use the example only to illustrate the distinction; develop angles appropriate to the actual product being researched.
Investigate potential angle categories, including:

* Pain-point relief.
* Desired outcome or transformation.
* Convenience and time savings.
* Comfort and ease of use.
* Performance or effectiveness.
* Cost savings or long-term value.
* Avoidance of waste or regret.
* Emotional relief.
* Confidence and peace of mind.
* Identity and self-expression.
* Social signaling or gifting.
* Novelty and curiosity.
* Problem prevention.
* A specific use case or occasion.
* An underserved customer segment.
* A common alternative that fails to meet the need.
* A misunderstood product feature.
* A common misconception about the problem.
* A before-and-after contrast.
* A specific purchase trigger.
* Ease of adoption or reduced effort.

These are research directions, not mandatory categories. Discard irrelevant angles and develop additional ones if the evidence supports them.
For each promising angle, provide:

1. Angle name.
2. Target segment.
3. Core customer insight.
4. The situation in which the angle becomes relevant.
5. The problem, desire, or motivation being addressed.
6. The underlying functional, emotional, or social job.
7. Why this product is relevant to that motivation.
8. The specific feature or benefit supporting the angle.
9. The main promise or value proposition.
10. The evidence supporting the angle.
11. The primary objection it must overcome.
12. The type of proof needed to make the claim credible.
13. A sample headline.
14. A sample opening hook for a short-form video or advertisement.
15. A sample product-page value proposition.
16. A suggested creative concept or demonstration.
17. A suitable offer or call to action, if appropriate.
18. The principal hypothesis that a marketing test should validate.

Keep angles meaningfully differentiated. Changing only a few words in the headline does not create a new angle.
Prioritize differences in buyer motivation, problem framing, purchase context, desired outcome, or reason for choosing the product.

PHASE 8: MAP ANGLES TO CUSTOMER AWARENESS
For each priority segment, identify the customer's likely level of awareness:

* Unaware: They have not clearly recognized the problem or need.
* Problem-aware: They recognize the problem but may not know the available solutions.
* Solution-aware: They know the category of solution but have not selected a specific product.
* Product-aware: They know the product or comparable products and are evaluating them.
* Most-aware: They understand the product and primarily need a compelling reason or offer to buy.

For each relevant awareness level, determine:

1. What the customer already understands.
2. What they do not yet understand.
3. What information would be most persuasive.
4. Which sales angle fits the stage.
5. What type of creative, demonstration, explanation, or proof is appropriate.
6. What objection needs to be resolved before the next step.

Do not force every product or segment into every awareness stage.
Explain how the same product could require a different opening message for a person who does not recognize the problem versus a person comparing two competing products.

PHASE 9: OBJECTIONS, TRUST, AND PROOF
Identify the main reasons a potential customer might hesitate.
Consider:

* Price.
* Product quality.
* Uncertainty about effectiveness.
* Difficulty of use.
* Compatibility with the customer's needs.
* Durability.
* Shipping or return concerns.
* Lack of trust.
* Skepticism toward marketing claims.
* Availability of cheaper alternatives.
* Lack of urgency.
* Fear of purchasing the wrong product.

For each significant objection, provide:
* The customer concern.
* Why the concern exists.
* The segment most likely to have it.
* The evidence that would resolve it.
* The appropriate response or reassurance.
* The proof that should appear in marketing.
* Any product limitation that should be acknowledged honestly.

Do not invent guarantees, certifications, endorsements, performance claims, clinical benefits, or customer results.

PHASE 10: RANK THE ICPs AND SALES ANGLES
Create a prioritization framework for the discovered customer segments and sales angles.
Evaluate each on a 1–5 scale using the following criteria:

* Strength of evidence supporting the segment or angle.
* Severity or importance of the customer problem.
* Strength of the desired outcome.
* Urgency of the buying trigger.
* Relevance of the product to the need.
* Plausible willingness to pay.
* Ease of reaching the audience through available channels.
* Differentiation from competing offers.
* Ability to demonstrate the benefit credibly.
* Commercial attractiveness given available pricing and margin information.

Explain the criteria and scoring methodology.
Use the scores to prioritize experiments, not to imply statistical certainty. Mark unavailable information as unknown rather than assigning arbitrary scores.
Present:

* The top three customer segments, if supported.
* The top five sales angles, if supported.
* The strongest angle for each priority segment.
* The most promising angle for initial testing.
* The best alternative angle if the first test fails.
* The key evidence gaps that could change the ranking.

Explain why each leading angle outranks the alternatives.

PHASE 11: TURN RESEARCH INTO A TESTING PLAN
Create a practical validation plan for the leading ICPs and angles.
For each test, specify:

1. Hypothesis.
2. Target customer segment.
3. Sales angle.
4. Creative concept.
5. Example hook.
6. Core message.
7. Product proof or demonstration.
8. Call to action.
9. Primary metric.
10. Secondary diagnostic metrics.
11. What result would support the hypothesis.
12. What result would weaken the hypothesis.
13. The next step based on the outcome.

Distinguish between tests that compare different audiences and tests that compare different messages within the same audience.
Where possible, hold the audience, offer, and other important variables constant while comparing angles.
Do not declare an angle successful based solely on clicks if the objective is profitable purchases. Consider conversion rate, customer acquisition cost, average order value, return rates, and contribution margin when the necessary data is available.
If baseline data and traffic estimates are missing, recommend an appropriate testing approach without inventing sample sizes, confidence levels, or expected performance.

PHASE 12: FINAL DELIVERABLES
Organize the final report in the following order.

A. Executive summary
Explain what the product is, its most compelling potential customer needs, the most promising buyer segments, and the strongest initial sales-angle opportunities.

B. Product intelligence report
Summarize the features, benefits, use cases, differentiators, and limitations.

C. Market and competitor findings
Summarize the competitive landscape, alternatives, customer complaints, and opportunities for differentiation.

D. Voice-of-customer findings
Provide the recurring customer problems, desires, objections, emotional motivations, and representative customer language, with source links.

E. Customer segment comparison
Create a table comparing the identified segments by situation, primary need, buying trigger, desired outcome, objections, and evidence strength.

F. Product-specific ICP profiles
Provide a detailed profile for each priority segment, including the person, situation, job to be done, purchase motivation, buying readiness, and poor-fit indicators.

G. Buyer journey and decision psychology
Explain how customers move from recognizing a need to considering a product and making a purchase.

H. Sales-angle library
Create a table with the following columns:
* Angle.
* Target segment.
* Customer insight.
* Trigger.
* Desired outcome.
* Product benefit.
* Evidence strength.
* Main objection.
* Sample hook.
* Recommended test priority.

Follow the table with a detailed breakdown of the strongest angles.

I. Awareness-stage messaging
Map the best angles and messages to the awareness levels relevant to the product.

J. Objection-handling framework
Provide the most important objections, evidence-based responses, and proof requirements.

K. Ranked priorities
Identify the highest-priority ICPs, angles, and experiments, explaining the reasoning and uncertainty behind the ranking.

L. Testing roadmap
Provide a practical first-round testing plan and a sequence for further research and optimization.

M. Research limitations and open questions
Clearly state what remains unknown, which conclusions are hypotheses, what evidence is missing, and which additional data would most improve confidence.

N. Source list
Include links to the most useful primary sources, customer discussions, product pages, reviews, and research references.
