## 1. The 10 most decision-relevant findings

1. **Most of an ad's measurable impact on TikTok happens in the first 2–6 seconds, so the product must be in the
   first frames, not just in the words.** TikTok's Marketing Science says the first 6 s capture about 90% of ad-recall
   impact and about half arrives in the first 2 s (A, 2023; method not published). Its help center says to introduce
   the proposition in the first 3 s (A, Jun 2025). A MediaScience lab study for TikTok found exposures of 6 s or
   less still gave 38% of the recall of 20 s+ exposures (A, 2021, n=343). Facebook/Nielsen found the same pattern on
   mobile feeds (C, 2016–17). **Implication:** our 11-word spoken callout takes about 4 s to say (D arithmetic), so
   the first frame has to do the work visually: the dress on the body, with the boots, in motion.
2. **Self-relevance raises attention *and* scrutiny, so callout hooks need strong proof right after them.**
   When a message is personally relevant, people judge it on argument quality; when it is not, they judge it on
   cues such as the source (B, Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman 1981; Petty, Cacioppo & Schumann 1983). Self-referencing copy
   increases persuasion only when the arguments are strong (B, Burnkrant & Unnava 1995), and extreme
   self-referencing can backfire (B, Meyers-Levy & Peracchio 1996). Matching ads to the audience lifted clicks and
   purchases in large Facebook field tests (B, Matz et al. 2017; the comparison baseline is disputed). TikTok
   reports that a creator saying "you" in the first 5 s goes with higher purchase intent (A, 2023, no method).
   **Implication:** the situation callout is well supported; what follows it must be concrete, checkable product
   proof (cable texture, drape, length, warmth, doesn't cling), not vibes.
3. **Engagement that is not about the product does not sell.** On TikTok influencer ads, a measure of how much the
   *product* sits in the most engaging moments predicted sales lift; general engagement is not the same thing
   (B, Yang, Zhang & Zhang, *Marketing Science* 2025). Entertainment has an inverted-U relation to purchase intent, and
   entertainment *before* the brand appears did not help purchase intent (B, Teixeira, Picard & el Kaliouby 2014).
   **Implication:** make the dress the attention-getter itself; never open with a gag or curiosity bait that is
   unrelated to the dress.
4. **Surprise helps only when it is relevant to the product message.** Surprise and joy reduce zapping and focus
   attention (B, Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012). But unexpected *relevant* information improved attitudes, while
   unexpected *irrelevant* information hurt them (B, Lee & Mason 1999). Clickbait-style curiosity often lowers
   credibility without reliably raising clicks (B, small studies). **Implication:** pattern interrupts must be
   product facts ("it's a knit that doesn't cling when you sit"), not stunts.
5. **There is no credible evidence for an "attention reset every N seconds" rule, and faster is not automatically
   better.** In TV studies, more *related* edits raised arousal and memory without overload (B, Lang et al. 2000), but
   fast pacing combined with arousing content can overload processing (B, Lang et al. 1999, LC4MP). A 2026 experiment
   on a 20-s talking-head clip found higher cut frequency *reduced* sustained engagement (B-, conference paper, n=242).
   The "every 3 seconds" rule appears only in vendor blogs with no primary source (C). **Implication:** our 4 shots in
   15 s (about one cut every 3.75 s) is a reasonable middle; test it, don't speed it up by default.
6. **The no-captions rule probably costs us recall, and costs all of the message for anyone watching muted.**
   TikTok's own guidance recommends captions or text overlays (A, Jun 2025; A, 2023: captions linked to +31%
   likeability, +95% brand affinity, no method). Same-language subtitles raised brand recall, verbal memory and
   behavioural intent in TV ads *with sound on* (B, Brasel & Gips, *JAMS* 2014). Short key-term text beats verbatim
   text, which can impair learning (B, Adesope & Nesbit 2012 meta-analysis; Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013). Muted share on
   TikTok is not credibly measured: TikTok says sound is vital to 88–90% of users (A, survey), while a 2019 US survey
   found 69% watch video sound-off in public (C). About 15% of US adults report some trouble hearing (official NIDCD
   statistic). **Implication:** the owner may want to reconsider; the cheapest test is 3–5 key words (not a
   transcript) on screen versus none (section 4).
7. **Faces help attention, and where the face looks steers attention to or away from the product.** A face or a
   person early in the ad goes with better viewership and brand lift (C, Google 2015 analysis of thousands of
   YouTube ads; A, TikTok 2023: person in first 2 s +50% "hooking power"). Eye-tracking experiments show a face looking
   *at the product* increases attention to and memory of the product and brand; a face looking *at the viewer* holds
   attention on the face (B, Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014; Adil et al. 2018). **Implication:** direct gaze for the
   callout, then the presenter looks at and touches the dress during the demo shots.
8. **Brand early raises recall but can raise skips; brief repeated brand moments beat long ones.** Google's analysis
   found brand in the first 5 s raised recall and awareness but also skips (C, 2015). Spreading the same brand
   exposure into short "pulses" reduced zapping (B, Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2010). Showing the brand early and often,
   with at least one spoken mention, aids recall (B review, Romaniuk 2009). TikTok says branding in the hook should be
   subtle (A, Creative Codes 2023). **Implication:** the product is visible from frame 1; the name "Ember & Clove" is
   said once mid-ad; with no on-screen text, the spoken brand mention is the *only* brand cue, so it must be there.
9. **Lo-fi, native, person-led creative is TikTok's consistent advice, but AI-made ads carry a trust and memory
   risk.** TikTok says lo-fi, people-led, "TikTok-first" ads drive more actions and recall (A, 2021–2023, commissioned,
   little method). NIQ's 2024 neuromarketing study found viewers often spotted AI-generated ads, found them less
   engaging, and showed weaker memory activation even for high-quality ones (C, n>2,000, EEG on ~150). AI-disclosure
   studies are mixed: labels lower perceived authenticity and trust in several experiments, but can add novelty
   (B-, mostly small or student samples). **Implication:** let the product demonstration carry credibility rather than
   the presenter's testimony; never script the AI presenter as a customer.
10. **Attention, memory, clicks and purchases are different outcomes, and creative can win one and lose the next.**
    Ad liking predicted split-cable sales better than persuasion scores in the ARF validity project (B, 1991,
    contested). Creativity has robust positive effects, strongest for high-involvement products (B, Rosengren et al.
    2020 meta-analysis). Clicks correlate with people who would buy anyway, so observed click returns overstate causal
    effects (B, Blake, Nosko & Tadelis 2015). **Implication:** judge hooks on qualified outcomes (destination CTR, CVR
    on clicks, add-to-cart), using stop rate and retention as diagnostics only, as section 6 of the Checkpoint A
    report already sets out.

## 2. The science, section by section

### 2.1 The first 0–3 seconds

**Platform facts first (A).** TikTok's help center (Jun 2025) says to put the hook in the first 6 s and the
proposition in the first 3 s, feature people, use captions or text overlays ("5-10 words per second"), and favour a
less polished look. TikTok's 2020 auction-ad analysis says over 63% of the highest-CTR videos show the key message or
product in the first 3 s (no sample size given). Creative Codes (May 2023) adds: subtle branding in the hook; the
suspense hooks it measured lifted watch time by 16%, surprise was linked to 1.7× view-through rate, and emotional
openings to 1.7× awareness (Lumen for TikTok, 2021). Treat all of these as directional: TikTok publishes no method,
sample or comparison baseline.

**Entertainment vs purchase intent (summary).** Techniques that *show the product doing something the buyer cares
about* (demonstration, progression, relevant surprise, education) build purchase intent. Techniques that hold eyes
for their own sake (fast cuts, irrelevant surprise, humor and music not tied to the product, curiosity bait) mainly
build entertainment and can lower purchase intent if they come before or instead of the product
(Teixeira 2014; Yang 2025; Lee & Mason 1999).

**On "attention reset every N seconds".** We found no peer-reviewed, TikTok-published or transparently measured
industry evidence for any fixed interval (3 s, 2 s or otherwise). The claim circulates in editing-tool blogs that cite
unnamed "internal studies" (C, unsourced). What the evidence supports is weaker and conditional: cuts trigger
orienting (B); more *related* cuts can help memory up to some point (B, TV); fast pacing with arousing content or
too-frequent transitions can hurt (B). Treat cut rate as a variable to test, with retention *and* CTR as outcomes.

### 2.3 Cognitive load and video comprehension

**Core model.** LC4MP (Lang 2000): viewers have limited processing capacity; structural features (cuts, onsets,
voice changes) automatically pull resources to encoding, but too many features plus arousing content exceed capacity
and memory for the central message drops (Lang et al. 1999). Mayer's multimedia principles, from lab learning
studies, give the practical rules below. Learning studies use instructional material, not ads, so transfer to a 15-s
ad is plausible but not proven (D).

| Element | What the evidence says | Evidence | Rule for us |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Spoken dialogue** | Spoken words with pictures beat written words with pictures (modality effect; Ginns 2005, 43 studies, n=1,887; a later 91-study analysis found smaller effects, d≈0.24). TikTok: sound-on lifts "communicating new information" by 55% (A, Kantar 2021, survey). Faster speech yields more affective, less cognitive processing (Megehee, Dobie & Grant 2003). | B, A | Keep about 30–38 words per 15 s (D, at ~2.5 words/s); natural pace; one claim per shot. |
| **Captions / on-screen text** | Same-language subtitles raised brand recall, verbal memory and intent (Brasel & Gips 2014); verbatim text with narration can impair learning, small discrepancies help (Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013); text + speech beats speech alone, mostly through key terms, and helps low-knowledge viewers (Adesope & Nesbit 2012). TikTok recommends captions or overlays (A). | B, A | If the owner allows text: 3–5 key words, not verbatim, never more than one line at a time. |
| **Background music** | Extraneous music reduced retention and transfer in two experiments (Moreno & Mayer 2000). Congruent music helps (Oakes 2007). TikTok says music lifts awareness (A). | Mixed | No-music rule is defensible. If ever tested, use quiet, congruent music under the voice, not a track that competes with it. |
| **Scene complexity** | Dense, cluttered visuals ("feature complexity") lowered brand attention and ad attitude; well-designed creative complexity raised comprehension and attitude (Pieters, Wedel & Batra 2010, 249 ads). | B | Plain apartment backgrounds; one hero object (the dress); boots as the only prop. |
| **Product demonstrations** | Demonstration supports mental simulation (Elder & Krishna 2012). Under cognitive load, process simulation breaks down and progression loses its advantage (Cian 2020). | B | Show one demonstrable property per shot (texture, stretch, drape/no cling, styling). |
| **Editing speed** | See 2.2: related edits can help memory; fast pacing + arousing content can overload; no fixed optimum. | B | 4 related shots; every cut adds information. |

**Can over-stimulation hurt trust, recall or conversion?** Evidence says yes for comprehension and recall
(Lang 1999; Moreno & Mayer 2000; Pieters et al. 2010). For trust, the evidence is indirect: clickbait-style framing
lowers credibility (B, small studies); unexpected-irrelevant content lowers attitudes (Lee & Mason 1999); and
NIQ (C) linked AI-generated ads to "confusing" and weaker memory. For conversion, we found no controlled study that
isolates stimulation level on short-form purchases; it is a hypothesis to test (D).

### 2.4 Attention versus persuasion

| Outcome | What makes a video succeed here | Can fail at the next stage because… | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Entertaining** (watched, liked, shared) | Joy, surprise, humor, story, native feel | Entertainment unrelated to the product, or placed before the brand, does not lift purchase intent; too much lowers it | B (Teixeira 2012, 2014); C (Google 2015) |
| **Memorable** (brand and product recalled) | Product on screen, brand early and in pulses, at least one spoken brand mention, text redundancy | Recall without a reason to buy; early brand raises skips | B (Romaniuk 2009; Teixeira 2010; Brasel & Gips 2014); C (Google 2015); A (TikTok 2023) |
| **Persuasive** (attitude, intent) | Strong arguments for high-relevance viewers; cues (source, likability) for low-relevance ones (ELM); demonstration and simulation | Intent ≠ action; the AI label may weaken source cues (trust) | B (Petty et al. 1981, 1983; Elder & Krishna 2012; AI-disclosure studies) |
| **Click-causing** (destination CTR) | Clear offer and CTA; curiosity; relevance | Curiosity-bait clicks bounce; clicks over-represent people who would buy anyway | B (Blake et al. 2015); C (TikTok diagnostics, Checkpoint A §7) |
| **Profitable purchase** | Product–buyer match; truthful demo (fewer returns); price and page match the ad; enough margin | Attribution can credit the ad for sales it didn't cause; small samples mislead | B (Gordon et al. 2019 and Lewis & Rao 2015, in Checkpoint A); D |

**Why a creative can win one stage and lose another (mechanisms).**
- *ELM split:* a callout makes the dress personally relevant, so weak arguments then hurt more than they would with a
  generic ad (B). A charming presenter persuades low-relevance viewers through cues, but those viewers rarely buy (D).
- *Ad liking:* liking predicted split-cable sales in the ARF validity project (B, 1991), but reanalyses and
  individual-level studies dispute that it is causal (B). Likable ≠ selling, but disliked ads rarely sell.
- *Long vs short:* Binet & Field's IPA analysis (996 campaigns) argues short-term activation metrics can undersell
  brand building and recommends roughly 60:40 brand:activation (C/B-, observational databank, not TikTok). For a new
  DTC store on a small test budget, activation is the right focus now, but memorable branding still matters (D).
- *Creativity:* creativity effects are robust and larger for high-involvement products; consumers, not award juries,
  should judge it (B, Rosengren et al. 2020, 878 effect sizes). Apparel at $45–53 is moderate involvement (D).
- *Weak link to success:* in about 10,000 Estonian brand TikToks, 77 coded structural features gave only "modest but
  consistent" prediction gains, and most features were unrelated to success (B-, arXiv 2026, preprint). Expect most
  creative "rules" to matter less than product, offer and audience.

## 3. Rules for maintaining attention without overwhelming

Each rule lists its basis. Rules built on B and A evidence are defaults; rules resting on D are things to test.

1. **The product is the hook.** Frame 1 shows the dress worn, in motion, filling most of the frame (B Pieters &
   Wedel 2004; A TikTok 3-s guidance; B Yang 2025).
2. **Say the situation in at most ~3 s.** Callouts of about 8 words fit; 11+ words spill past the window (D arithmetic;
   A TikTok first-2-s impact).
3. **Show the situation too.** If the line says "knee-high boots", the boots are on screen in frame 1, so muted
   viewers get the callout visually (D; B modality).
4. **One idea per shot, and each cut adds product information** (B Lang 2000 related edits; B Pieters 2010).
5. **No cut for its own sake; no fixed reset interval.** Four shots in 15 s is the default; test 3 vs 5 rather than
   assuming faster wins (B Lang 1999; B- Dost & Huang 2026; C unsourced reset claims).
6. **Surprise only with product facts** (B Lee & Mason 1999; B Teixeira 2012).
7. **Strong, checkable arguments right after the callout** (B ELM; B Burnkrant & Unnava 1995).
8. **Gaze steers attention:** direct gaze for the callout, eyes and hands on the dress during demos
   (B Sajjacholapunt & Ball 2014; B Adil et al. 2018).
9. **Brand in pulses:** product visible throughout, "Ember & Clove" spoken once around 6–10 s, maybe once more in the
   CTA; no long logo hold (B Teixeira 2010; B Romaniuk 2009; C Google 2015).
10. **Entertainment after, not before, the product** (B Teixeira 2014).
11. **Uncluttered scene:** one hero garment, one prop that serves the callout (B Pieters, Wedel & Batra 2010).
12. **Speak at a natural pace, ~30–38 words per 15 s;** no claims the page can't back up (D; B Megehee 2003).
13. **If text is ever allowed, key words only, not a transcript** (B Adesope & Nesbit 2012; B Yue et al. 2013).
14. **Judge by qualified outcomes:** stop rate and the per-second curve diagnose; destination CTR, CVR on clicks and
    add-to-cart decide (A metric definitions, Checkpoint A §6; B Blake et al. 2015).

## 4. Applications to Ember & Clove's 15-s AI try-on format

### 4.1 A default 15-s timeline (D, built from the evidence above)

| 0.0–3.0 s | 1 | Presenter already wearing the midi **with knee-high boots**, mid-step or turning (motion onset), full-length, plain wall, direct gaze | "Own knee-high boots? This dress is for you." (8 words) | Visual + spoken callout within the 3-s window; motion onset; direct address |
| 3.0–7.5 s | 2 | Close on hands running over the cable knit and ribbed waist; gaze on the dress | "Cable knit, mock neck, fitted through the hips." | Demonstration; argument quality for high-relevance viewers. bought" or "I've worn this for years".

### 4.3 What the no-captions rule likely costs (plainly)

- **Muted viewers get no words at all.** Our ads have no text and no music, so with sound off the only message is
  the image. How many TikTok viewers are muted is not credibly measured: TikTok reports 88–90% say sound is vital and
  73% would "stop and look" at sound-on ads (A, survey, 2020–21), while a 2019 US survey of 5,616 adults found 69%
  watch video sound-off in public (C, all video, not TikTok). About 15% of US adults report some trouble hearing
  (NIDCD; lower among younger adults). So the cost is real but of unknown size.
- **Sound-on viewers lose a recall boost.** In TV ads with audio, same-language subtitles drew gaze and raised brand
  recall, verbal memory and behavioural intent (B, Brasel & Gips 2014). Young adults increasingly watch with
  subtitles by preference (C, YouGov 2023: 63% of US under-30s prefer them on for TV).
- **TikTok itself recommends text** (A, Jun 2025 help center; A, 2023 captions +31% likeability, +95% affinity,
  no method).
- **The case for the rule.** Redundant verbatim text can impair processing (B, Yue et al. 2013; Mayer redundancy),
  and a clean, text-free frame looks more like native UGC (D). These support "no transcript-style captions", not
  "no text at all".
- **Lowest-cost options the owner could consider:** (1) a single key-word overlay of 3–5 words for the first 3 s
  only (segment + product, e.g., "Knee-high boots? Cable knit midi"); (2) enabling TikTok's native auto-captions on
  the organic post, which viewers can switch off. TikTok's 2021 newsroom describes this feature; whether captions
  now show by default, and whether they appear on Spark Ads, needs checking in the app (*verify*).

### 4.4 Other places the evidence questions current rules

| Rule | What the evidence says | Suggested action |
|---|---|---|
| **First line names the buyer by situation** | Well supported (self-relevance, ELM, targeting). But our example line runs ~4 s, past the 3-s window TikTok stresses (A); muted viewers miss it (no text). | Keep the rule; shorten the line to ≤ ~8 words and show the situation visually. Test long vs short callout. |
| **No music** | Mixed: TikTok claims music lifts awareness and creator-ad purchase intent (A); lab and Google evidence say extraneous or early music can hurt comprehension or brand awareness (B, C). | Keep as default; a later, low-priority test of quiet congruent music under the voice. |
| **AI presenter** | TikTok's creator and "real people" lifts (A) were measured on human creators; NIQ found AI ads less engaging with weaker memory traces (C); disclosure effects on trust are mixed (B-). TikTok requires the AI label (A, Checkpoint A). | Keep labelled; make the dress demonstration carry the credibility; consider a future test against real try-on footage of the actual garment (Phase 5). |
| **4 shots in 15 s** | Consistent with evidence; no support for a faster default. | Keep; test 3 vs 5 later. |

### 4.5 Test hypotheses (prioritised)

Budget reality from Checkpoint A: about $25/day and roughly 5 purchases a week at best, so purchase-level winners
cannot be declared from these tests. Primary metrics are therefore upstream but qualified (destination CTR, CVR on
clicks, add-to-cart), with stop rate (2-s views ÷ impressions) and the per-second retention curve as diagnostics.
One variable per comparison; same product, segment, audience settings and budget in each arm.

| ID | Hypothesis | Variable | Mechanism | Primary metric | Secondary | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| H-AP-01 | A key-word overlay (3–5 words, first 3 s) raises destination CTR versus no text | Text overlay on/off | Muted reach + redundancy recall | Destination CTR | Stop rate, 3-s retention, ATC | B Brasel & Gips; A TikTok |
| H-AP-02 | A short callout (≤ 8 words) keeps more viewers to 3 s than the 11-word callout | Callout length | 3-s window; earlier proposition | Retention at second 3 (per-second curve) | CTR | A TikTok first 2–6 s; D |
| H-AP-03 | Motion-onset first frame raises stop rate versus static first frame | First-frame motion | Orienting to onset | 2-s stop rate | 100% view rate, CTR | B Abrams & Christ; B LC4MP |
| H-AP-04 | Presenter gazing at/touching the dress in shot 2 raises CTR versus gazing at camera | Gaze direction | Gaze cueing to product | Destination CTR | Second 4–8 retention | B Adil 2018; B Sajjacholapunt & Ball |
| H-AP-05 | A situation callout gives lower stop rate but higher CVR on clicks than a generic benefit opener | Callout vs benefit opener | Self-relevance, qualified traffic | CVR (clicks), ATC per 1,000 impressions | Stop rate | B ELM, Matz 2017; D |
| H-AP-06 | A relevant-surprise opener (sit-down "no cling" demo first) beats the callout opener on CTR for the cling-averse segment | Opener type | Expectation violation (relevant) | Destination CTR | Stop rate | B Lee & Mason; B Teixeira 2012 |
| H-AP-07 | Progression (dress → boots → coat) beats a single before/after cut on CTR | Transformation format | Process simulation | Destination CTR | 100% view rate | B Cian 2020 |
| H-AP-08 | Brand said once at ~8 s vs. in the first line: early brand raises recall but lowers 3-s retention | Brand timing | Brand early vs skips | Retention at second 3 | Branded search / direct traffic (directional) | C Google 2015; B Teixeira 2010 |
| H-AP-09 | 4 shots vs 6 shots (same script): no improvement from faster cutting | Cut count | Pacing, load | 100% view rate and CTR | Per-second curve | B Lang 1999/2000; B- Dost & Huang 2026 |
| H-AP-10 | Quiet congruent music under the voice does not change CTR versus no music | Music on/off | Arousal vs. load | Destination CTR | Completion | Mixed A/B/C (low priority; owner rule) |

Suggested order: H-AP-02 and H-AP-03 (cheap, within current rules), then H-AP-01 (needs owner sign-off on text),
then H-AP-05/06 (segment strategy), then the rest.

## 6. Open questions

1. **What share of TikTok ad impressions is watched muted in the US?** No independent figure found; TikTok's numbers
   are attitude surveys from 2020–21. Our per-second curve can't see mute state. Only a text-vs-no-text test (H-AP-01)
   can estimate the *cost* for us.
2. **Do TikTok's creator, face and "you" lifts hold for a labelled AI presenter?** All TikTok figures came from human
   creators. Phase 5 should look for 2025–26 studies; our own tests may be the best evidence.
3. **Do TikTok auto-captions show by default, and on Spark Ads?** Needs checking in the app before proposing it as a
   compromise (*verify*).
4. **How short can a situation callout be before it loses self-relevance?** No study on callout length; H-AP-02.
5. **Is there any credible cut-frequency optimum for 15-s ads?** None found; H-AP-09 is exploratory.
6. **Does a callout lower stop rate but raise purchase quality?** Theory says plausibly (D); at ~5 purchases a week we
   will see this only directionally (CVR on clicks, add-to-cart).
7. **Full texts not read:** Brasel & Gips (sample sizes, sound conditions), Dost & Huang (PDF unreadable), Lang et al.
   1999, TikTok's underlying Kantar/Lumen reports. Confidence on their details is capped accordingly.
8. **Music:** TikTok's evidence (A) and lab evidence (B) disagree; only our own test would settle it for this format,
   and it is low priority given the owner's rule.
